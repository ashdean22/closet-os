# Styling Rules & Principles

Reference knowledge for assembling outfits. These are guidelines, not laws — the
user's actual closet always takes priority. If a rule can't be satisfied with the
items available, satisfy as many as possible and note the compromise rather than
recommending an item the user does not own.

---

## A. Core Color Rules

1. **Three-color maximum.** Limit an outfit to roughly three colors: one dominant/base, one secondary, one accent. More than three reads busy unless deliberately maximalist.

2. **60-30-10 distribution.** Aim for ~60% dominant color (usually the largest garments), ~30% secondary, ~10% accent (shoes, belt, bag, small layer). This creates balance without calculation.

3. **Neutrals are free.** Black, white, grey, beige, cream, tan, camel, navy, olive, and denim behave as neutrals — they pair with nearly anything and don't count toward the three-color limit.

4. **Anchor every outfit.** At least one garment should be a neutral, giving the eye a place to rest. Two saturated colors with no neutral anchor usually reads chaotic.

5. **Monochrome works through texture.** An all-one-color outfit (navy on navy, cream on cream) is inherently cohesive, but needs varied fabric textures to avoid looking flat or like a uniform.

6. **Tonal dressing.** Different shades/tints of one hue (light blue + navy + slate) is easier to pull off than exact-match monochrome and always reads intentional.

7. **Analogous colors harmonize.** Colors adjacent on the wheel (blue + green, red + orange, yellow + green) share undertones and blend naturally. Safest way to use two real colors.

8. **Complementary colors contrast.** Opposites on the wheel (navy + burnt orange, olive + burgundy, blue + orange, purple + yellow) create energy. Use one as dominant and one as accent — a 50/50 split is jarring.

9. **Split-complementary is softer.** One base color plus the two colors flanking its complement gives dynamic contrast with less tension than direct complements.

10. **Desaturate for wearability.** Any color scheme becomes more wearable in muted, dusty, or deeper versions. Rust instead of orange, sage instead of green, burgundy instead of red.

11. **Don't mix warm and cool whites.** Ivory/cream and stark white in the same outfit look accidental unless clearly separated by another layer.

12. **Match undertones for neutral-on-neutral.** Pair warm neutrals together (camel + cream + tan) and cool neutrals together (grey + white + charcoal). Mixing warm and cool neutrals can look muddy.

13. **Shoes carry disproportionate weight.** They're small in area but anchor the whole look. A clashing shoe color undermines an otherwise balanced outfit. Default to a neutral or repeating a color already present.

14. **Repeat a color for cohesion.** Echoing one color in two places (e.g. a belt and a shoe, or a shirt and a jacket lining) makes an outfit look deliberately styled.

15. **Black and navy together is fine.** Once considered a mistake, now standard — but they read best with obvious contrast in texture or a third element separating them.

16. **Denim is a neutral.** Blue jeans and denim jackets pair with virtually any color, including other blues in a contrasting wash.

## B. Pattern & Texture

17. **Vary the scale.** When mixing two patterns, pair a large-scale print with a small-scale one. Two large or two similar-scale patterns compete.

18. **Share a color.** Two patterns work together when they have at least one color in common — that shared hue is what makes it read intentional rather than accidental.

19. **Two patterns maximum for most outfits.** Three is possible but advanced; anything more requires a very deliberate maximalist intent.

20. **Break patterns with solids.** When mixing prints, keep accessories and at least one garment solid so the eye has a resting point.

21. **Stripes are a neutral pattern.** Thin stripes, subtle checks, and small polka dots behave almost like solids and mix easily with bolder prints.

22. **Texture creates interest without color.** In a monochrome or all-neutral outfit, contrasting fabrics (chunky knit + smooth leather, denim + silk, wool + cotton) supply the visual variety color would otherwise provide.

23. **Contrast texture weight.** Pair a heavy or rough texture with a smooth or fine one — chunky knit with slim trousers, leather with soft cotton.

24. **Limit the palette when mixing textures.** Multiple textures work best within a tight color range; multiple textures plus multiple bold colors becomes noisy.

25. **Place bold patterns where you want attention.** A patterned top draws the eye up; patterned bottoms draw it down.

26. **Match formality of fabric to occasion.** Wool, silk, fine cotton, and leather read formal. Denim, jersey, fleece, canvas, and corduroy read casual. A single casual fabric can undercut an otherwise formal outfit.

## C. Proportion, Fit & Silhouette

27. **Rule of thirds.** Divide the body roughly 1/3 to 2/3 rather than 50/50. A cropped or tucked top with high-waisted bottoms gives 1/3 top ÷ 2/3 bottom; a longline jacket over slim trousers gives 2/3 top ÷ 1/3 bottom. Both read resolved; an even split reads unresolved.

28. **Offset volume.** If one half is oversized or flowing, the other should be fitted or tapered. Volume on volume loses the silhouette entirely.

29. **Avoid the mid-thigh stop.** Tops or layers ending at the widest part of the hip/thigh create an awkward horizontal break. Go clearly shorter or clearly longer.

30. **Define the waist to add structure.** A belt, tuck, or cinched layer creates a visual break that makes long or loose silhouettes look intentional rather than shapeless.

31. **Long silhouettes need an interruption.** A maxi dress or full-length coat benefits from a belt, seam, or contrasting layer to avoid reading as one uninterrupted column.

32. **Matching shoe to trouser color elongates the line.** Continuous color from waist to floor visually lengthens; a contrasting shoe interrupts it.

33. **Fit outranks everything.** A well-fitting inexpensive garment beats a poorly fitting expensive one. When two options are equal, prefer the one described as tailored or fitted for formal contexts.

34. **Layer in odd numbers where possible.** Three visible layers (e.g. tee + shirt + jacket) tends to look more considered than two.

35. **Layers should graduate.** Each successive layer should be equal or longer/heavier than the one beneath, or clearly intentionally cropped over it.

36. **Structured pieces raise formality.** A blazer, tailored coat, or collared shirt immediately elevates casual components — the fastest lever for moving an outfit up the formality scale.

## D. Formality & Dress Codes

37. **When uncertain, dress up.** Being slightly overdressed reads as respect; underdressed reads as indifference.

38. **The lowest-formality item sets the ceiling.** Sneakers with a suit caps the outfit at smart casual regardless of the suit. Identify the least formal piece to know what the outfit actually reads as.

39. **Formality scale (low → high):** athleisure → casual → smart casual → business casual → business professional → cocktail → black tie → white tie.

40. **Casual.** Clean, comfortable, intentional. Jeans, tees, sneakers, casual outerwear. Not sloppy — no visible damage unless deliberately styled.

41. **Smart casual.** Casual foundation with one or more elevated elements: a blazer over a tee, dark denim with leather shoes, a knit polo. No sweatpants, no athletic sneakers, no distressed items.

42. **Business casual.** Workplace-appropriate but no suit required. Chinos or tailored trousers, button-down or knit, optional blazer, leather shoes or clean minimal sneakers. Tie optional/usually absent.

43. **Business professional.** Suit or coordinated separates in dark neutrals, dress shirt, leather dress shoes, belt matching shoes. Conservative colors and minimal pattern.

44. **Cocktail.** Elegant but not floor-length. Dark or mid-tone suit for men (tie optional); knee-length dress or dressy separates for women. Room for texture and subtle pattern.

45. **Black tie.** Tuxedo, white dress shirt, bow tie, patent or polished black shoes for men; long gown or formal cocktail-length dress for women. No exceptions improvised from a casual closet — if the closet can't support it, say so.

46. **Athleisure.** Athletic pieces styled deliberately: matching or coordinated sets, clean sneakers, structured outer layer. Requires at least one non-gym element (a nice jacket, quality knit) to read as styled rather than as workout clothes.

47. **Interviews default one level above the workplace's daily norm.** When in doubt for an interview, business casual is the safe floor.

48. **Evening skews darker and richer.** The same formality level reads better in deeper tones, richer fabrics, and more metallic/reflective accents at night than during the day.

## E. Weather & Practicality

49. **Weather beats aesthetics.** A stylistically perfect outfit that leaves the wearer cold, soaked, or overheated has failed. Prioritize appropriate coverage and materials.

50. **Rain:** favor leather or treated footwear over suede or canvas; darker colors on the lower half; a water-resistant outer layer. Avoid light-colored hems and delicate fabrics.

51. **Cold:** layer for insulation (base + mid + outer). Wool, fleece, down, and knits over thin cottons. Note that layering also creates the visual interest cold-weather outfits need.

52. **Heat:** favor breathable natural fabrics (linen, cotton), lighter colors, looser cuts. Avoid synthetics and heavy layers.

53. **Transitional temperatures (55–70°F):** a light layer that can be removed is the key piece — a shirt jacket, cardigan, unlined blazer, or denim jacket.

54. **Match footwear to terrain and activity,** not just to the outfit. Long walking, standing, or uneven ground overrides style preference.

## F. Style Aesthetics (recognize and stay consistent)

55. **Minimalist:** clean lines, neutral palette, simple silhouettes, no visible branding, quality basics. Fewer elements, better fit.

56. **Streetwear:** oversized fits, graphic tees and hoodies, statement sneakers, caps, cargo or relaxed pants. Black/white/grey base with bold accent pops.

57. **Grunge:** flannel, distressed denim, band or washed tees, combat boots, layered and oversized, muted or dark washed palette. Deliberately un-polished.

58. **Classic / old money:** tailored fits, natural fabrics, muted neutrals, minimal logos, structured outerwear, leather shoes. Timeless over trendy.

59. **Boho:** flowing fabrics, earth tones (rust, mustard, olive), layered textures, natural materials, artisan or vintage accessories, relaxed silhouettes.

60. **Athleisure:** technical and knit fabrics, coordinated sets, clean sneakers, minimal accessories, function-forward.

61. **Vintage / retro:** era-specific silhouettes and prints. Best executed with one or two period pieces grounded by modern basics — a full period costume reads as costume.

62. **High fashion / avant-garde:** experimental proportion, asymmetry, unconventional layering, monochrome or extreme contrast. Requires deliberate commitment; half-measures read as mistakes.

63. **Preppy:** polos, oxford shirts, knitwear over shoulders, chinos, loafers, stripes and checks, navy/white/red palette.

64. **Stay in one lane per outfit.** Mixing two aesthetics generally requires a shared element — a consistent color palette, silhouette, or repeated detail — or the outfit reads as indecisive rather than eclectic.

---

## G. Assembly Priorities

When the closet is limited, resolve conflicts in this order:

1. **Occasion appropriateness** — formality must be right or nothing else matters.
2. **Weather suitability** — comfort and practicality.
3. **Color cohesion** — neutrals and anchoring.
4. **Proportion and fit.**
5. **Pattern/texture interest.**
6. **Aesthetic consistency.**

**Always:** build only from items the user actually owns. Never invent, assume, or recommend a garment not in the retrieved set. If a role (shoes, outerwear) can't be filled from the closet, state it as missing rather than substituting something inappropriate.
